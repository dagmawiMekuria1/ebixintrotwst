Annuities

What is an annuity?

An annuity is a financial product sold by insurance companies. You give them a sum of money — either all at once or over time — and they pay you a regular income stream, typically during retirement.

In simple terms, an annuity is the reverse of life insurance. Life insurance protects your family if you die too soon. An annuity protects you if you live a very long time and risk running out of money.

The two phases

Every annuity has two main phases:

  • Accumulation phase — This is when you are putting money in. Your contributions grow on a tax-deferred basis, meaning you do not pay taxes on the gains until you start taking money out.
  • Payout phase (annuitization) — This is when you start receiving payments. You can choose to receive income for a set number of years or for the rest of your life.
Key Takeaway

Annuities turn savings into guaranteed income. They are best suited for people nearing retirement who want predictable monthly payments they cannot outlive.

Types of annuities

The two most common types differ in how your money grows:

  • Fixed annuity — The insurance company guarantees a minimum interest rate on your money. Your payments in retirement are predictable and stable. Lower risk, lower potential return.
  • Variable annuity — Your money is invested in sub-accounts similar to mutual funds. Payments can go up or down depending on market performance. Higher risk, higher potential return.

There are also indexed annuities, which tie returns to a stock market index (like the S&P 500) but usually include a cap on gains and a floor that protects against losses.

Things to watch out for

  • Surrender charges — If you withdraw money early (typically within the first 5–10 years), you may pay a penalty fee.
  • Fees — Variable annuities in particular can have high annual fees, including mortality and expense charges, investment management fees, and administrative costs.
  • Tax treatment — Withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income, not at the lower capital gains rate. Withdrawals before age 59½ may also incur a 10% IRS penalty.
