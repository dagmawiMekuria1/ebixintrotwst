Health Insurance

How health insurance works

Health insurance helps you pay for medical care. You pay a monthly premium to your insurance company, and in return, they cover a share of your medical expenses — from doctor visits and prescriptions to hospital stays and surgeries.

Without health insurance, even a short hospital stay can cost tens of thousands of dollars. Insurance spreads that risk across a large pool of people, so no single person bears the full cost alone.

Key cost terms

Health insurance involves several types of costs. Understanding these makes it much easier to compare plans:

  • Premium — The fixed monthly amount you pay to keep your coverage active, whether or not you use any medical services.
  • Deductible — The amount you pay out of pocket before your insurance starts paying. For example, with a $1,000 deductible, you pay the first $1,000 of covered services yourself.
  • Copay — A flat fee you pay for specific services, like $25 for a doctor visit or $10 for a generic prescription.
  • Coinsurance — After you meet your deductible, you and your insurer split costs by percentage. An 80/20 plan means the insurer pays 80% and you pay 20%.
  • Out-of-pocket maximum — The most you will pay in a year. Once you hit this limit, insurance covers 100% of covered services for the rest of the year.
Key Takeaway

Plans with lower premiums usually have higher deductibles and copays. Plans with higher premiums tend to cover more upfront. Choose based on how often you expect to need care.

Plan types: HMO vs. PPO vs. EPO

Most health insurance plans fall into one of three categories, each with different rules about which doctors you can see:

  • HMO (Health Maintenance Organization) — You must choose a primary care physician (PCP) and get referrals to see specialists. You can only use doctors within the HMO network. Lower premiums, less flexibility.
  • PPO (Preferred Provider Organization) — You can see any doctor without a referral, but you pay less if you stay in-network. Higher premiums, more flexibility.
  • EPO (Exclusive Provider Organization) — Similar to a PPO, but you must stay in-network (except for emergencies). No referrals needed. A middle ground on price and flexibility.

What does “in-network” mean?

Insurance companies negotiate discounted rates with certain doctors, hospitals, and labs. These providers are called in-network. When you visit an in-network provider, you pay less because the insurer has pre-negotiated the price.

Out-of-network providers have not agreed to those discounts, so your share of the cost is much higher — and some plans won’t cover out-of-network visits at all.
